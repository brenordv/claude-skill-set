# User interviews

Build a customer-discovery interview script that gets honest answers instead of polite lies, plus
the framework to synthesize the results. The method follows The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick): ask
about past behavior, never about hypothetical intent, and never pitch before the reaction phase.

Input needed: the product idea and the assumptions to validate. Assume the founder has never run
a discovery interview.

## 1. Interview objectives

State the top 3 assumptions the interviews must test. For each: the assumption phrased as a
testable hypothesis, what a validating answer sounds like, and what an invalidating answer sounds
like. If the founder cannot articulate assumptions, derive them from the idea and say so.

## 2. Screening

- Must-have criteria: 3-4 attributes an interviewee needs for their answers to count.
- Nice-to-have criteria: 2-3 attributes that make a session more valuable.
- Disqualifiers: who to exclude and why (friends and anyone invested in the founder's success
  give polite data).
- Where to find them: name specific communities, LinkedIn search terms, and recruiting tools
  (UserInterviews.com and Respondent are the usual paid routes).
- Incentive: a concrete recommendation (gift-card amount or product access) sized to the
  audience's hourly value.

## 3. The script

Write exact words to say, not paraphrases. Target a 25-minute conversation.

**Opening (2 min).** Set expectations, ask for honest and negative feedback explicitly, and get
recording permission with a one-line consent ask.

**Warm-up (3 min).** 2-3 questions about their role and context that lead naturally into the
problem space without revealing what the founder is building.

**Problem exploration (10 min).** 5-7 questions probing whether the problem exists, how severe it
is, and what they do about it today. Rules: ask about past behavior ("tell me about the last time
you..."), never mention the solution, no leading questions, no hypotheticals. Give each question a
follow-up prompt.

**Solution exploration (5 min).** 3-4 questions on how they would solve the problem, asked before
showing anything. Listen for features they name unprompted, their priority order, and
dealbreakers.

**Reaction (5 min).** A 2-sentence description of the concept (write those sentences), then 3-4
follow-ups to gauge the reaction, with the specific signals that separate real interest from
politeness (see the cheat sheet below).

**Closing (2 min).** Ask for referrals to similar people, run a commitment test ("would you be
willing to [small concrete action] this week?"), thank them, state next steps.

## 4. Analysis framework

For synthesizing 5-8 interviews:

- A pattern-detection table: one row per interviewee, one column per assumption, cells holding the
  verbatim evidence, so patterns are visible at a glance.
- Signal vs. noise: a pattern needs independent confirmation from at least 3 interviewees; one
  vocal outlier is an anecdote.
- Decision criteria: state up front how many confirmations move the verdict to build, pivot, or
  kill, so the founder cannot move the goalposts after the fact.

## 5. Red-flag cheat sheet

Polite, not honest: "that sounds cool" with no specifics; "I would definitely use that" (future
tense is unreliable); "you should add feature X" (they are designing for you, not describing their
problem); they cannot recall the last time they hit the problem.

Genuine interest: they describe the problem in specific, emotional terms unprompted; they ask when
they can use it; they offer introductions to others with the same problem; they accept the
commitment test.
